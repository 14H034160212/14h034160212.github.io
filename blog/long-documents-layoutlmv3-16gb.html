Long documents with LayoutLMv3 on a 16 GB GPU, without pre-training

LayoutLMv3 and ERNIE-Layout read at most 512 text tokens. A page of an order form or an invoice with many line items is often longer, and everything after token 512 is never seen. On the three real datasets below, cutting pages at 512 tokens hides 41% to 58% of the words the model has to label.

In short: letting the models read the whole page, with no extra pre-training, gives 20 to 33 more F1 points on all three datasets. It runs on a 16 GB GPU: inference at 2048 tokens needs 0.80 GB, against 0.69 GB at 512.

Results

Each model is fine-tuned on each dataset in the usual way (2,000 steps, 5 random seeds). The only difference between the columns is what happens to text beyond 512 tokens: cut off (the baseline), read in overlapping 512-token windows, or read in one pass with the model's positions extended.

The datasets: VRDU (Google), with TV advertising orders and US registration filings; and the FCC Invoices of RealKIE, 370 real invoices of several pages each, licensed CC BY-NC 4.0. Popular invoice and receipt datasets such as FATURA and SROIE are too short to test this: none of their labels lies beyond 512 tokens.

How it works: two small changes

1. Let the model read more than 512 tokens. The model learned 512 position vectors, one per position. I repeat them: position 513 reuses the vector of position 1, and so on, then fine-tune as usual. This is the trick Longformer used. It works because the model can still tell the repeats apart from each word's position on the page and from the distances between words, which it also uses. No pre-training is needed, and fine-tuning learns as fast as before: the whole-page model is ahead of the baseline at every checkpoint and passes the baseline's final score after a quarter of the training.

2. Keep the memory down. At every layer, LayoutLMv3 adds a score for each pair of words based on how far apart they are. The standard implementation computes this for every pair with large temporary arrays, and that, not the text itself, is what fills the memory on long pages. The score depends only on the distance, and there are only a few thousand distances. So I compute it once per distance, look it up, and share it across layers. The results are exactly the same as the original model's: on real data, all four implementations I compared gave the same F1 and the same prediction for every word.

Training at 2048 tokens (batch 2) needs 4.0 GB with gradient checkpointing, and 4096 tokens fit in 6.7 GB. Batches are padded only to their longest page, so short pages cost the same as before. Training on the order forms at 2048 tokens took 1.5 times as long as the baseline, not 4 times.

What did not help

  • Stretching the positions instead of repeating them (position interpolation) was 0.7 to 1.4 points worse. It shifts every word's position, even on short pages, so the model loses what it learned; the loss was largest on pages of 1024 tokens or fewer.
  • ERNIE-Layout's own long-sequence variant (rotary positions) in its PyTorch port dropped to 28 F1, because it throws away the learned positions.
  • Padding ERNIE-Layout on the right, as its original PaddleNLP code does, gave the same accuracy as the port's left padding after fine-tuning.

Details for practitioners

V100. I measured on an A100 limited to 15 GB and to the kernels a V100 supports, not on a real V100. The lookup version uses PyTorch's built-in memory-efficient attention, which runs on V100 tensor cores. I also tried FlexAttention, which keeps memory flat even at 8192 tokens (0.87 GB) and is fast on an A100. It compiles with Triton, though, which no longer uses tensor cores on pre-Ampere GPUs, so I don't recommend it for a V100.

What is new. Repeating position embeddings (Longformer, 2020), converting models to long inputs with sparse attention (LSG, 2022), extending embedding models without training (LongEmbed, 2024) and exact relative-position attention kernels (arXiv 2611.01182, 2026) all exist. What I have not seen elsewhere is the combination for layout models: their 1D and 2D position scores, no pre-training, exact attention, plain PyTorch kernels that run on a V100, and evidence on real long pages.
